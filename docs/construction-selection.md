## Candidate A — Pasini–Vaudenay, PKC 2006

### What the paper constructs

Pasini and Vaudenay define a generic SAS-based key-agreement transformation. It takes an underlying authenticated key-agreement protocol (AKA0) and a message cross-authentication (MCA) protocol. The MCA authenticates the exchanged protocol transcript and the final messages of AKA0; the participants then complete AKA0. Their Theorem 1 bounds the composed attack probability by the sum of the AKA0 and MCA attack probabilities, with a constant security-time overhead. The construction is not an arbitrary KEM wrapper.

The proposed three-move MCA appears in Figure 4 and Theorem 3. Alice has input message `m_A`; Bob has input message `m_B`. Its exact randomized commitment is:

```text
K  ← uniform {0,1}^κ
e  ← uniform {0,1}^ℓe, independently
c  = H(e, K, m_A)
d  = (e, K)
```

Here `H` is the paper’s random oracle with an `ℓ_c`-bit output. Alice sends `(m_A, c)`. Bob chooses independent uniform `R ← {0,1}^ρ` and sends `(m_B, R)`. Alice opens `c` with `d`, computes `SAS = R ⊕ h_K(m_B)`, and sends that SAS through the authenticated short-string channel. Bob checks the corresponding SAS, checks `m_A ≠ m_B` to prevent the paper’s stated reflection attack, and authenticates the SAS back. Successful outputs are the peer identity and the peer’s authenticated message. The opening value `e` is independent commitment randomness; replacing this commitment with a different hash formula is not the paper’s construction.

The hash family `h` is assumed `ε_h`-almost strongly universal, with `ρ`-bit digests. In the theorem’s stated model, if an attacker launches at most `Q` Alice or Bob instances and makes at most `q` queries to `H`, the MCA success probability is bounded by:

```text
ε = q²·2^(-ℓe) + 13q²·2^(-ℓc)
Pr[successful MCA attack] ≤ Q(Q−1)/2 · (2^(-ρ) + ε + ε_h)
```

The paper says this multi-instance bound is essentially tight in the relevant range. The `2^-ρ` term alone is therefore not the full theorem. Larger `Q`, `q`, or hash-family deviation worsens the bound. Applying Theorem 1 to the three-move AKA construction adds the separately established AKA0 attack probability; it does not erase it. For the paper’s DH instantiation, the AKA0 group and its security assumption must therefore be specified and analyzed as well. The paper gives estimates relating `ρ`, network size, allowed runs, and a target attack probability; those estimates are not a universal retry policy or a project-selected SAS target.

The proof uses a Bellare–Rogaway-style model with active control of insecure-channel messages, launches of concurrent protocol instances, test/reveal/corrupt queries, and an authenticated narrowband channel for short messages. The paper’s AKA definition explicitly sets aside inconsistent Alice/Bob end states by assuming mutual authentication is performed by further communication. Theorem 3 proves MCA security, not every property of the project’s ceremony and result contract.

### Paper-level assumptions and construction limits

- The 3-move MCA theorem is in the random-oracle model. It assumes the random-oracle commitment `H(e,K,m_A)`, including fresh independent `e`; an almost strongly universal hash family `h`; and the stated query and instance bounds. The authors say a standard hash may instantiate the random oracle only if that random-oracle instantiation is justified.
- The three-move AKA is obtained by composing that MCA with an AKA0, and the paper discusses classical Diffie–Hellman. Theorem 1 is generic over a secure AKA0, but is not a proof that an arbitrary contemporary KEM is a secure AKA0 or that an arbitrary KEM adaptation preserves the reduction.
- The model assumes an authenticated short-string channel. It does not quantify real human comparison errors, partial comparison, accidental approval, accessibility, or localization.
- The result is key agreement and message/peer authentication under that model. The paper does not specify the project’s exact ceremony identifier, context encoding, persistent attempt store, cancellation policy, or application result contract.
- Security claims do not transfer to an implementation merely because it is described as “based on” PV.

## Shortcake is not the analyzed paper construction

As accessed 2026-09-27, [Meta’s Shortcake repository](https://github.com/facebook/shortcake/tree/db73640a5531b5266bd1094d72e947ef22295cc1) was at `main` commit `db73640a5531b5266bd1094d72e947ef22295cc1`, tagged `v0.1.0-pre.4`. Its README says the implementation has not been audited. The current source describes a generic KEM protocol: the Initiator generates a KEM keypair and a 32-byte nonce, commits to the encapsulation key and nonce, the Responder encapsulates to that key and generates a nonce, and the Initiator reveals its nonce.

Shortcake’s commitment is a domain-separated hash over the encapsulation key and initiator nonce with length-prefixed fields (`shortcake-commitment-v1`, in `src/commitment.rs`). It does not use the PV paper’s independent `e` and committed random `K` in `H(e,K,m_A)`. Its SAS is the responder nonce XOR a hash of the initiator nonce and ciphertext; its session key is derived from the KEM shared secret and transcript. That is an implementation design, not evidence that the PV theorem covers this KEM construction or commitment. Shortcake itself warns that the session key must not be used until SAS verification, and its SAS bytes are caller-truncated. These observations are implementation facts, not security proof.

## Candidate B — Beskorovajnov and Müller-Quade, ACNS 2026

### Publication versions

The conference paper is Wasilij Beskorovajnov and Jörn Müller-Quade, “How to Kickstart \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SMT}}^{S\rightarrow R}\) with Short Authentication Strings and Out-of-Band Communication,” ACNS 2026, LNCS 16571, pp. 415–443, DOI [10.1007/978-3-032-32560-0_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-32560-0_15), first online 2026-07-22. The complete author manuscript is [IACR ePrint 2025/1598](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1598), revised 2025-09-11; its title is “How to kickstart Secure Message Transfer with Short Authentication Strings and Out-Of-Band Communication.”

The publisher abstract and accessible Appendix A, Theorem 5, state the same one-sided UC authentication result with \(\varepsilon=2^{-t}\) and the same commitment, random-oracle, and OOB assumptions described below. The full ePrint manuscript supplies the detailed one-sided, mutual, and SMT proofs (Theorems 2–4). The Springer page exposes the publication record and theorem excerpt, but its complete chapter PDF was not accessible here; no claim of full-text identity between versions is made. No material conflict appears in the accessible theorem statements, so the complete ePrint version is the source for the detailed mapping.

### What the paper constructs

This is not a direct reusable key-agreement protocol. Section 4 first proves UC realizations of authenticated-message functionalities, then Section 5 composes mutual SAS authentication with a one-shot, sender-to-receiver KEM/DEM secure-message-transfer protocol. The paper’s abstract and formal definitions distinguish the authentication building block from the final message-transfer functionality.

The synchronous OOB functionality \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{OOB}}^{\mathrm{SAS}}\) receives `(SAS, sid, s)` from each honest endpoint, reveals those values to the network adversary, compares the two strings exactly, and immediately returns the equality bit to both endpoints. The adversary cannot suppress this comparison result. The paper assumes honest endpoints and uncompromised display/software for this idealization.

For one-sided \(\pi_{\mathrm{SAS}}\), the caller/environment supplies `sid`, or the Sender samples a fresh \(\kappa\)-bit `sid` and notifies the Receiver over the main channel. The Sender commits to `(sid || m_S)` with fresh commitment coins `r_S`, the Receiver sends fresh uniform `r_R`, and the Sender opens the commitment. If the opening verifies, both calculate:

```text
s = Trunc_t(H(sid || m_S || r_S || r_R))
```

Both submit `(SAS, sid, s)` to the OOB functionality. On equality, the Receiver outputs `m_S`; otherwise it aborts. The mutual \(\pi_{\mathrm{SAS}}^{\times}\) also sends `m_R` with `r_R`; the SAS input is `sid || m_S || m_R || r_S || r_R`. On equality each side outputs the other side’s message; on mismatch both abort. Thus roles and arbitrary caller-supplied context can be bound by their fixed positions as `m_S` and `m_R`, subject to the caller’s encoding and semantics.

The proof assumes a computationally hiding and binding commitment and models `H` as a random oracle. The paper says its main constructions instantiate `Commit` as hash-then-open in that model; the protocol uses `Commit(sid || m_S; r_S)` and opening witness `d`. The Sender’s `r_S` commitment coins and Receiver’s independent \(\kappa\)-bit `r_R` both enter the SAS input. This is not PV’s `H(e,K,m_A)` commitment.

Theorems 2 and 3 state that the one-sided and mutual protocols UC-realize their respective authenticated-channel functionalities with a per-session misbinding parameter \(\varepsilon=2^{-t}\), for one online forgery attempt per `sid`. This exact parameter is the ideal functionality’s Bernoulli gate; the UC real/ideal distinguishing bound additionally contains the commitment hiding/binding advantages, a negligible pre-reveal random-oracle hit term (bounded in the proof by \(2q_H2^{-\kappa}\)), and negligible terms.

Theorem 4 composes \(\pi_{\mathrm{SAS}}^{\times}\) with a fresh receiver KEM keypair, a one-time DEM, and a fresh sender signature keypair. The cross-authentication inputs are the sender’s signature verification key and receiver’s KEM public key. Sender-to-receiver payload ciphertexts are signed over a context containing the protocol name, `sid`, and direction; the Receiver verifies the signature before decapsulation/decryption. Under IND-CPA security of the KEM, one-time OT-CPA security of the DEM, EUF-CMA security of the signature, and the SAS-authentication theorem, the paper gives:

```text
Adv_UC ≤ 2^(-t) + Adv_EUF-CMA(Signature)
                  + Adv_IND-CPA(KEM) + Adv_OT-CPA(DEM) + negl(κ)
```

This is a one-shot secure message-transfer result with length-only leakage. It is not an application API that returns a reusable shared pairing key to both parties. The paper’s alternative MAC construction in Appendix A explicitly leaves its proof for future work and is not used as evidence for the theorem.

### OOB, SID, and lifecycle boundaries

The paper separates cryptographic misbinding probability from human reliability. It explicitly discusses an \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{human}}\) extension for misreads, inattentive comparison, or UI confusion, but sets \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{human}}=0\) in its proofs and leaves calibration to future work. Accordingly, \(2^{-t}\) is the cryptographic failure probability conditional on faithful comparison of all displayed bits, not a deployed-system error rate.

The formal `sid` is selected by the environment/caller or sampled by the Sender when absent; it is carried in the commitment tag, SAS hash input, OOB calls, authentication functionality, and SMT signed context. The proof is subroutine-respecting and has per-`sid` state; UC composition supports concurrent sessions with distinct session identifiers. The SID is not itself a peer identity or proof of a fresh ceremony across application restarts. The application still has to allocate unique, shared SIDs and prevent stale state or approval from being reused.

The ideal authentication functionalities allow one forge attempt per `sid`, and the SMT functionality similarly has one misbinding attempt before delivery. They do not impose a global budget over arbitrarily many SIDs, cooldowns, durable counters, or process-restart policy. The OOB functionality is synchronous and immediate. The paper sketches an asynchronous functionality with replay-resistant approvals and peer/session descriptors but explicitly leaves its formal treatment out of scope. Network messages can be delayed or dropped; invalid openings, unequal SAS, failed verification, and decapsulation errors abort. The paper does not define P1’s cancellation, expiry, continuation, or durable terminal-state contract.

The security theorems assume honest S and R and do not model endpoint corruption. No long-term identity keys are needed in the canonical SMT variant: KEM and signature keypairs are fresh per session. The ephemeral sender signature authenticates signed ciphertexts, but this does not establish proof-of-possession or ownership of a consumer’s long-term identity key. The KEM/DEM result uses the paper’s classical PPT notions; it does not by itself claim post-quantum security or validate an arbitrary implementation.

### Construction, output, and assumptions

Candidate C is the paper’s specific three-round encryption-based construction, `Enc-AKA`; it is not generic over arbitrary AKEs and does not use a DH exchange plus a SAS-MCA wrapper. In its `Enc-MCA` parent, the initiator `P_i` has a public/private key pair for a CCA-secure encryption scheme. The protocol assumes permanent `PK_i` / `SK_i` across sessions and a globally selected commitment parameter in the common-reference-string model. `Enc-AKA` is the specialization `m_i = null`, `m_j = K`: responder `P_j` samples a new uniform session key `K ∈ {0,1}^ℓ` for each run.

1. **Initiator:** sample fresh `R_i ∈ {0,1}^k` and nonce `s_i ∈ {0,1}^ℓ`; commit to `[m_i | s_i | PK_i | R_i]`; send `(m_i, s_i, PK_i, c_i)` over the attacker-controlled network. For `Enc-AKA`, `m_i` is null.
2. **Responder:** sample fresh `R_j ∈ {0,1}^k`, nonce `s_j ∈ {0,1}^ℓ`, and session key `K`; encrypt `[m_j | s_j | R_j]` under the received `PK_i` using the CCA-secure public-key encryption scheme; send ciphertext `e_j`. For `Enc-AKA`, `m_j=K`.
3. **Initiator and responder:** initiator decrypts `e_j`, opens `c_i`, and sends the decommitment; initiator computes `SAS_i = R_i ⊕ R_j`, responder computes `SAS_j = R_i ⊕ R_j`, and they exchange these k-bit values on the SAS channel. Each accepts only if its equality check passes. The initiator outputs the decrypted `K`; the responder outputs its sampled `K`.

The protocol also computes a session identifier from a collision-resistant hash of the sent and received transcript, including SAS messages; the paper says the proof also works with the concatenated fresh nonces `[s_i | s_j]`. The SID is local output, not a separately negotiated wire field. In the security model a session is locally indexed; two sessions are matching when peers and roles are reciprocal, and partnered when their transcript SIDs agree. Fresh random nonces make a session partner unique except with negligible probability. The formal AKA output is `(Peer, K, sid)`. Correctness says partnered sessions both accept and output the same key.

Theorem 1 proves the parent Enc-MCA’s message authentication; Theorem 2 states Enc-AKA’s key-security result. Given a `(T_C, ε_C)` non-malleable commitment and a `(T_E, ε_E)` single-secret CCA-secure encryption scheme, its attack bound is:

```text
Adv ≤ 2 n τ_t τ_c (2^-k + max(ε_C, ε_E))
T ≤ min(T_C, T_E) - μ
```

Here `n` is the number of players, `τ_t` bounds total sessions per player, `τ_c` bounds concurrently live sessions for each pair, and `μ` is a small constant overhead. Theorem 2’s game tests a partnered session key against random; the proof also uses Theorem 1’s cross-authentication result to rule out an accepted wrong key. The bound is not just `2^-k`: it includes the factor 2, session counts, and primitive advantages. The paper gives ROM hash-commitment parameters in terms of the hash query budget; it chooses no project-wide SAS length, total-attempt budget, or operational parameter.

The cited instantiations are OAEP-RSA in the ROM under the RSA assumption, and a randomness-reusing CCA-secure ElGamal variant under the DH assumption in the ROM. Vanilla ElGamal, plain ECDH, and a KEM are not interchangeable instantiations. The generic theorem can apply to a public-key encryption scheme only when that exact scheme satisfies the paper’s CCA notion and the commitment assumption. Turning a KEM into a KEM-DEM public-key encryption scheme would need its own composition argument under the required notion; the paper gives no post-quantum claim.

### Enc-MCA messages and context binding

The Enc-MCA inputs `m_i` and `m_j` are arbitrary strings. Section 2 describes the encrypted plaintext space as `[m | R]`, where `m` is an adversarially chosen string; Figure 2 commits to `[m_i | s_i | PK_i | R_i]` and encrypts `[m_j | s_j | R_j]`. Callers can represent structured values as bytes, but the paper specifies no structured encoding, field semantics, or validation rule.

Enc-MCA outputs the peer’s authenticated message on each side. Successful authentication means the peer actually sent that message in some session, except for the Theorem 1 attack bound; it does not mean that the two application messages are equal or meet an application policy. Both messages participate in the transcript-derived session identifier. Changing either message therefore changes the transcript and, except for a hash collision, the SID. An attacker’s substitution is an authentication event covered by Theorem 1’s bound. If a peer itself sends a different value, Enc-MCA authenticates and returns that value rather than rejecting it; the caller must check it against its expected input.

Roles are part of the formal instance model: matching sessions require reciprocal peer inputs and opposite initiator/responder roles, and partnering also requires equal SIDs. The ordered protocol messages and transcript SID distinguish the two message positions. This supports formal peer, role, and per-session binding for Enc-MCA’s authenticated messages. It does not give those messages application meaning or prove that the key output by Enc-AKA is bound to them.

### Why Theorem 2 does not cover application context

Figure 2 and the Enc-AKA definition fix `m_i = null` and `m_j = K`, with the responder sampling a fresh uniform `K` for each session. Theorem 2’s proof first reduces an initiator accepting a key different from the responder’s session key to the Enc-MCA authentication reductions, then reduces distinguishing that responder key from random to SS-CCA security. Its challenge ciphertext contains the fresh key, and its security game tests the key output by a partnered session. The proof does not state a theorem parameterized by extra application context or changes to either Enc-AKA message.

The proof relies on these exact roles for the values:

- `m_i = null` is the fixed initiator input in the Enc-AKA protocol being proved. The proof does not analyze a variable initiator application-context value.
- `m_j = K` makes the responder’s fresh uniform `K` exactly the value encrypted to the initiator and output as the session key. In the SS-CCA reduction, the challenge ciphertext contains one of two candidate keys; after the Enc-MCA reductions rule out acceptance of a different key, distinguishing the tested output from random breaks SS-CCA.
- The Enc-MCA reductions and SAS equality checks justify delivery/authentication of that encrypted responder value in a partnered session. They do not ensure that both endpoints reach local output when SAS delivery is delayed or dropped.

Theorem 1 alone cannot fill this gap: it proves authenticity of arbitrary Enc-MCA messages, not secrecy of an Enc-AKA key jointly bound to those messages. Nor does the paper prove that running Enc-MCA beside Enc-AKA, or changing the two Enc-AKA inputs to include context, preserves Theorem 2. The desired substitution/composition therefore requires a new proof. No KDF or other custom composition is inferred here.

P1 may eventually require binding generic protocol/domain identifiers, profile versions, roles, ceremony identifiers, opaque context from each participant, and any public-key claim the result intends to authenticate. This list does not select fields or an encoding. Enc-AKA’s theorem does not include these context inputs. Formal peer, role, and SID partnering does not establish that two context-extended sessions sharing a key agree on those values. The paper neither proves nor supplies a counterexample for a state such as “A: `K` with B and context X; B: `K` with A and context Y”; it leaves that context-confusion question outside its theorem. A context value separately authenticated by Enc-MCA is not thereby proven bound to the Enc-AKA key.

### Terminal outputs and key confirmation

Each participant outputs locally after its own SAS equality check. In Figure 2 the initiator outputs after receiving the responder’s SAS, while the responder outputs after receiving the initiator’s SAS. The paper permits SAS messages to be delayed or dropped. If one SAS message is delivered and the other is dropped, one participant can output `K` while the other remains incomplete. Theorem 2’s correctness statement is conditional: if sessions are partnered, both accept and output equal keys. It does not require every local accept to have a partnered terminal accept.

The SAS checks provide directional protocol-level evidence that the peer processed corresponding protocol data. Candidate C itself has no final acknowledgement authenticated under `K`. This is a limit of the selected paper construction, not proof that confirmation cannot be composed after it.

### Generic key-confirmation result

Fischlin, Günther, Schmidt, and Warinschi formalize **full** and **almost-full key confirmation** and account for which role receives the final protocol message (S&P 2016, Definitions 3.1–3.5, Theorem 5.1). Theorem 5.1 states that for a key-exchange protocol with Match security and key secrecy, a generic post-exchange transformation using a secure PRG-style KDF and a key-only unforgeable MAC preserves those properties and the protocol's authentication property, and yields full key confirmation for the server role and almost-full confirmation for the client role. The transformation derives separated confirmation keys and a refreshed output key, and exchanges role-directed MACs over the session identifier. It is a published composition result, not a protocol design inferred here.

Candidate C has the relevant shape: its SAS-AKA model defines peer, reciprocal roles, transcript session identifiers, partnered sessions, concurrent executions, and a key-indistinguishability test (SCN 2010, Sections 3.2–3.3; Theorem 2). Its full-version proof shows that an accepted initiator cannot obtain a key different from the responder's fresh `K`, and reduces key indistinguishability to the SS-CCA assumption (Contemporary Mathematics full version, Section 5). This supports applying the generic theorem's key-confirmation result to Candidate C at the AKE interface, with roles mapped to client/server and the existing transcript SID used as the session identifier. Candidate C does not itself instantiate the transformation.

The resulting confirmation is intentionally asymmetric. The final-message receiver can have **full key confirmation**: it knows a partnered peer session already accepted the same key. The final-message sender has **almost-full key confirmation**: a corresponding session with the agreed key-confirmation identifier exists, and if it accepts it must hold the same key, but the sender cannot know the final message arrived. The theorem does not provide common knowledge of success or atomic application commit. P1's equality-of-success-results property remains meaningful when stated conditionally: if both partnered sessions report success, they agree on key and partner/session. It must not require both sides to report success despite arbitrary message loss.

**Key-confirmation classification: ESTABLISHED COMPOSITION EXISTS**, for the narrow AKE key-confirmation property under Theorem 5.1's Match-security, key-secrecy, KDF, and MAC assumptions. This does not authenticate extra application context absent from Candidate C's session identifier, establish durable ceremony state, or turn local application completion into atomic bilateral success. Long-term identity proof-of-possession remains outside this pairing-key claim unless a result claims ownership of such a key.

### Security terms used in this audit

- **Key agreement / partnered-session correctness:** if two sessions meet the protocol's partnering relation, they accept and output the same key. Candidate C states this conditional correctness property in Section 3.3; it does not claim every accepting session has a partner.
- **Implicit key authentication:** key secrecy plus mutual authentication means no party other than the expected peer can obtain the session key, but the expected peer may not yet have accepted it.
- **Key confirmation:** evidence that another session holds the key. FGSW16's definitions are deliberately modular and do not by themselves identify that session as the intended peer.
- **Explicit key authentication:** FGSW16 explains that implicit key authentication plus key confirmation yields assurance that the expected peer holds the key. This is not the same as both applications committing.
- **Full / almost-full confirmation:** full confirmation requires a partnered session to have already accepted the same key; almost-full requires the corresponding session to share the key-confirmation identifier and, if it accepts, use the same key. The final-message sender necessarily gets the weaker guarantee.
- **Explicit mutual confirmation:** both directions exchange confirmation evidence. It still cannot make the final sender know the last message was received when delivery can fail; the endpoint guarantees remain role-asymmetric.
- **Agreement on data / session agreement:** agreement on a particular value (such as context or transcript) is a separate property from key secrecy and key confirmation. A session identifier/partnering relation is the formal rule that identifies corresponding sessions; it does not automatically include application values.
- **Injective agreement:** an accepted run corresponds to one unique peer run, preventing one peer run from being reused to justify multiple acceptances. Candidate C's fresh transcript SID and partnering are intended to make partners unique with overwhelming probability within its formal model.
- **Application success:** a local consumer's decision to return a successful result. It is a local state transition, not a cryptographic theorem unless the result contract maps it to a proved property.
- **Common knowledge of success:** each participant knows success, each knows the other knows, and so on without end. This is stronger than mutual confirmation and is unattainable in finite communication under arbitrary loss in the Halpern–Moses model.

### P1 terminal-semantics audit

Halpern and Moses, *Knowledge and Common Knowledge in a Distributed Environment*, JACM 1990, Section 8, Theorem 5, prove that common knowledge cannot be attained in a system where communication is not guaranteed, under their stated message-loss conditions. Their result concerns distributed knowledge, not cryptographic key exchange specifically; it applies to the coordination demand if P1 means that both endpoints must know that the other completed despite arbitrary loss. No finite acknowledgement chain removes the final-message uncertainty. Cryptographic protocols instead state local agreement and authentication guarantees, and may expose different acceptance/confirmation guarantees to the two roles.

**Classification: P1 REQUIREMENT IS AMBIGUOUS.** P1 says successful pairing requires “both participants [to] accept the same exact ceremony and compatible terminal outcome,” while its failure rules are local and its completion contract does not define what “compatible” means under loss. It can be read as an unattainable simultaneous/common-knowledge commit, but does not explicitly say that. No authoritative P1 text is changed in this research update.

Proposed clarification for P1:

- **Cryptographic result agreement:** if two sessions are partnered and both report success, they must agree on the same key, reciprocal peer and roles, exact ceremony/session, and every context value claimed authenticated by the protocol.
- **Local completion:** message loss may leave one endpoint complete and the other pending, failed, or canceled. The protocol must state each role's key-confirmation level; the two endpoints need not simultaneously know that both applications completed.
- **Safety:** a pending, failed, expired, or canceled ceremony cannot become successful due to a delayed/replayed message or stale approval for another ceremony.
- **Key confirmation:** the project should require the strongest role-specific confirmation justified by the composed proof. Under the cited generic transform, that is full confirmation for the final-message receiver and almost-full confirmation for the final-message sender. It does not require common knowledge.

This refinement changes only terminal semantics. It does not waive context agreement or cryptographic binding of context to the key/result.

### Contradiction search

- The common-knowledge impossibility might appear to rule out explicit confirmation entirely. FGSW16 contradicts that overbroad reading: full confirmation is achievable for the endpoint that receives the final confirmation message, while the sender gets the weaker almost-full notion. It does not establish symmetric/common-knowledge success.
- FGSW16 Theorem 5.1 may appear to solve context binding too because it authenticates the AKE session identifier. Its theorem only binds the session identifier defined by the underlying AKE; Candidate C's Enc-AKA SID does not include generic application contexts. Theorem 5.1 does not state a context-extended Enc-AKA result.
- Candidate C's Enc-MCA authenticates arbitrary messages, but Enc-AKA Theorem 2 fixes `m_i = null` and `m_j = K`. A separately authenticated context is not thereby proven to be bound to that AKE key. The earlier **NEW PROOF REQUIRED** context conclusion therefore stands.
- BFWW11 Theorem 1 composes BR-secure key exchange with a separately secure symmetric-key protocol when public session matching is available. This is contrary evidence to “no generic bridge exists,” but its precise BR game and secure-`π` premises have not been shown to match Candidate C's authenticated-SAS game and context-agreement need. It remains a proof lead, not an applicable theorem result.
- TLS and Bluetooth counterexamples show that a transcript hash, channel binding, user comparison, or secure component cannot be treated as a universal composition theorem. The result is limited to the exact standardized or proved interface.
- **Context split:** A may supply context X while B supplies Y. Candidate C's Enc-AKA theorem has neither input, so it does not rule out an application that associates those differing values with the same key; this remains the sole proof blocker.
- **Unilateral completion:** yes, one endpoint may finish its local SAS check while the peer's SAS message is delayed/dropped. Candidate C's correctness theorem only constrains partnered sessions; the key-confirmation transform gives the final receiver full confirmation and the sender almost-full confirmation.
- **Stale approval or restarted ceremony:** Candidate C's formal SID distinguishes modeled transcripts, but neither it nor FGSW's generic transform specifies restart-safe approval binding or durable terminal state. P1's stale-state safety rule remains necessary.
- **Cross-session confirmation and role swap:** FGSW's confirmation MAC covers the AKE session identifier and uses directional role keys; under the theorem's Match-security and MAC assumptions, a distinct session identifier or role cannot reuse the tag as valid confirmation. This does not bind context omitted from the SID.
- **Retries and old keys:** Candidate C's bound grows with its total-session and concurrent-session parameters, so repeated attacker-triggered runs increase the allowed failure bound and must count. `K` is fresh per analyzed run, but restart/persistence logic must still prevent an old local result from being relabeled as a new ceremony.
- **Application trust after partial completion:** A consumer could treat an endpoint-local output as durable peer trust even when the peer has not completed. Neither AKE theorem supplies application policy; the P1/result contract must expose local completion and confirmation level accurately.

The theorem’s symbolic parameters (`n`, `τ_t`, `τ_c`, `k`, `ε_C`, `ε_E`) do not require project-specific numeric values to decide whether this is a possible construction family. Any selected profile would still have to choose and enforce applicable limits and instantiate the assumptions. Theorem 2 assumes a non-malleable commitment in the CRS model and SS-CCA public-key encryption. The generic theorem does not itself assume the random-oracle model; the paper’s concrete hash-based commitment and cited OAEP-RSA and ElGamal instantiations do. Concrete primitives, SAS length, human comparison procedure, attempt values, wire format, and state machine remain profile decisions, not P2 blockers.

### Concurrency, human channel, and key reuse

Section 3 explicitly permits concurrent instances, including the timeout/restart situation where one device starts again while its peer remains in an older run. The attacker may interleave network messages and choose which session’s SAS message to deliver. Sessions have local indices, reciprocal peer/role matching, and transcript-derived SIDs; `τ_t` counts total sessions and `τ_c` counts live sessions per pair, and both enter the theorem bound. This is formal session separation, not a durable application ceremony identifier. The protocol does not store terminal SIDs, couple UI approval to them, survive application restarts with anti-replay state, or put consumer context into Enc-AKA (`m_i` is null). The introductory channel description permits replay of SAS messages; the formal `SAS-send` game removes each queued SAS message after delivery, so duplicate delivery of the same SAS message is not represented there. The insecure network does allow replay, delay, drop, modification, and injection.

The SAS channel is public, authenticated, and source/target authenticated for each ordered player pair. The attacker can read its values and can delay, drop, replay, and reorder them, but cannot modify or inject them. Section 3’s game permits observing, stalling, deleting, and reordering queued SAS values, while its consume-once queue does not model duplication. This is a formal authenticated-channel assumption; it is not a model of a person comparing displays. The paper gives no error model for misreading, partial comparison, blind approval, repeated-mismatch fatigue, accessibility, or rendering. Its cryptographic result therefore assumes the authenticated SAS channel works as defined and says nothing about real user error.

“Key re-use” means reusing long-term encryption key material across protocol sessions (including the same DH random contribution in the specialized ElGamal variant). It does **not** mean reusing the output pairing key: `K`, both k-bit `R_i/R_j` masks, both `s_i/s_j` nonces, and commitment coins are freshly sampled per ceremony. Encryption randomness is scheme-specific; the paper’s ElGamal instantiation explicitly reuses a DH random contribution. Reusing `SK_i` is central to the result’s efficiency and is explicitly non-forward-secret. The model uses static corruption and does not provide forward secrecy. Later compromise of a reused decryption secret can expose recorded responder ciphertexts and their past session keys, as well as compromise future runs; compromise of a reused DH exponent similarly exposes recorded shared values. The project has no stated requirement to reuse private keys, so this performance feature is not itself a reason to choose Candidate C.

### Exact DH-SC construction and result

The paper’s devices have preloaded public parameters for a prime-order subgroup \(G\) of \(\mathbb Z_p^*\), generator \(g\), and order \(q\). Alice and Bob independently choose exponents \(X_A,X_B\) from \(\{1,\ldots,q\}\), and compute \(g^{X_A},g^{X_B}\). They choose independent uniform \(k\)-bit strings \(N_A,N_B\), then form and commit to:

```text
m_A = 0 || ID_A || g^X_A || N_A
m_B = 1 || ID_B || g^X_B || N_B
(c_A,d_A) = commit(m_A); (c_B,d_B) = commit(m_B)
```

The four radio messages are `c_A`, `c_B`, `d_A`, `d_B`, in that order. Bob opens and checks Alice’s commitment and leading `0`, then computes `i_B = N_B XOR Nhat_A`. Alice opens and checks Bob’s commitment and leading `1`, then computes `i_A = N_A XOR Nhat_B`. Both devices display their locally computed `k`-bit string. The users compare the two displayed strings visually (the paper also allows vocal comparison). If equal, both accept the other’s DH public parameter and identifier and can compute the same DH value \(g^{X_AX_B}\). The paper says this exponentiation may be deferred; it does not define a KDF or a key-confirmation exchange. Figure 1 is the analyzed protocol; no modernized replacement is assumed.

Definition 1 and Theorem 1 concern authentication/integrity of the exchanged DH public parameters, not a modern AKE key-indistinguishability game. The theorem assumes an ideal commitment scheme, perfect random generation, uncompromised devices/users, fixed preloaded group parameters, and DDH hardness for the underlying passive DH secrecy argument. Its exact authentication bound is:

```text
Pr[attacker succeeds against any DH-SC run] <= n * gamma * 2^-k
Pr[attacker succeeds against one fixed target party] <= gamma * 2^-k
```

The first event ranges over any run, successful or abortive, among `n` users; `gamma` bounds each user’s total successful or abortive DH-SC sessions in its lifetime. This is a concrete authentication-event bound, not a key-distinguishing advantage and not just a per-run `2^-k`. No commitment or DH computational advantage is added to this formula: ideal commitment is assumed, while DDH is a separate underlying DH assumption. Section IV-B also assumes each party participates in at most one message-authentication session at a time. The paper therefore does not prove a concurrent-session bound. The theorem and assumption distinctions are visible in the author-hosted proceedings pages 470 and 474–475.

This differs from Candidate C’s `2 n tau_t tau_c (2^-k + max(epsilon_C, epsilon_E))`: C gives a partnered-session key test and explicitly counts total sessions per player and concurrent sessions per pair, with commitment and encryption advantages. DH-SC bounds authentication of DH parameters for any run, scales by lifetime sessions across the population, has no concurrency multiplier because concurrency is excluded, and idealizes the commitment. The two formulas measure different events and cannot be ranked by comparing their SAS terms alone.

### Human comparison, commitment, and DH assumptions

The paper’s intended OOB step is local display of `i_A` and `i_B`, followed by user equality comparison; nothing is typed. Its theorem treats the narrow authenticated comparison channel as ideal and assumes faithful, exact comparison of all `k` bits. It does not model misreads, partial checking, blind approval, accidental acceptance, repeated-mismatch fatigue, accessibility, or localization. The radio attacker sees and can modify, replay, schedule, or suppress radio messages. SAS values are displayed/compared out of band, not sent as radio messages; the model does not promise SAS confidentiality, and whether an attacker physically observes or delays a human comparison is outside the model. Modification/replay/suppression of the visual or vocal comparison/approval event is not separately modeled. Cryptographic success conditional on faithful comparison is not a measurement of real-world user reliability.

The commitment is **idealized in the proof**. Section II-C gives informal hiding and binding semantics and explicitly assumes an ideal commitment in the security analysis. Figure 1 commits to the role marker, human-readable identifier, DH public parameter, and random string. The paper does not give a concrete commitment construction, and its theorem does not say that any ordinary hiding/binding commitment can be substituted or analyze a hash commitment. Pasini’s 2009 thesis, Section 3.5.5, describes a generic ideal-commitment realization through a trusted third party, and notes that UC commitments in the CRS model can emulate an ideal commitment service. That is not a DH-SC-specific concrete reduction preserving this paper’s theorem: the TTP changes the trust model, and the thesis does not establish the CCH protocol as UC-secure in that ideal-functionality model. Pasini–Vaudenay’s concrete ROM commitment belongs to their separately analyzed PV-SAS-AKA, whose message flow and proof differ from Figure 1; it is not silently substituted here. A proof-preserving, trustless instantiation for this exact DH-SC result remains **NOT ESTABLISHED**.

The group is a prime-order subgroup and the generic security rationale is DDH hardness; the paper specifically describes multiplicative subgroups of \(\mathbb Z_p^*\) with preloaded parameters. Malicious-element validation and modern curve encodings are not specified. **Modern EC-DH applicability: STRAIGHTFORWARD THEOREM INSTANTIATION** at the abstract prime-order DDH-group level; a concrete curve/profile must separately meet the group and DDH assumptions, and its validation, encoding, and concrete security are not analyzed or selected here. No KEM result follows.

### Roles, identifiers, context, and ceremony binding

The fixed `0` / `1` markers are included inside the respective commitments. Section IV-D explains that the optimized, piggybacked MTSC exchanges otherwise permit reflection; the distinct markers and checks prevent that reflection. They establish Alice/Bob message-position separation in the protocol and map directly to initiator/responder roles. Neither role marker nor identifier enters the SAS formula: the locally displayed strings are only the XOR of the two random `N` values. The commitment/opening checks bind those random values to the role, identifier, and DH parameter that the user-authentication step accepts together. The committed `ID_A` / `ID_B` values are authenticated along with each party’s DH public parameter; the paper describes them as human-readable identifiers such as email addresses. They are not proof of possession of a long-term identity key or a verified real-world identity.

The underlying Section IV-B MTSC authenticator is parameterized by an arbitrary message `m`, and its proof authenticates the exact committed/opened message. Therefore, encoding additional context bytes into those authenticated messages is a **JUSTIFIED BY STRAIGHTFORWARD THEOREM APPLICATION** at the message-authentication layer, provided both parties check the exact expected context and preserve the commit-before-reveal ordering. The paper itself only specifies role, identifier, DH value, and nonce fields; it does not define structured context encoding, equal-context policy, a ceremony identifier, or a theorem specifically about application context bound to an output key. This is stronger than Candidate C’s result for arbitrary message authentication, but it does not create durable ceremony semantics.

There is no explicit SID field. Fresh `N_A` / `N_B`, commitment/opening state, and the proof’s local conversation indices distinguish analyzed executions; `gamma` counts abortive as well as successful lifetime attempts. The protocol does not model overlapping runs per party, process restarts, stale UI approvals, or durable replay state. The formal matching-conversation definition and active UM attacker include message replay and scheduling, but do not establish P1’s persistent ceremony-to-approval binding or arbitrary context semantics. A unique peer ID and role marker help bind the authenticated DH parameters; they do not by themselves prove application identity, UKS resistance for added context, or replay-safe application results.

### Terminal outcomes and key confirmation

Figure 1 says both users accept if the two displayed strings match, but there is no final network acknowledgement, per-endpoint approval exchange, or `K`-authenticated confirmation. Both openings must arrive before both devices can calculate their displayed value; if the last opening is delayed or dropped, one side may have a value while the other cannot complete the comparison. No radio message follows the human comparison, so the protocol cannot synchronize separate endpoint approval/terminal decisions: if one user declines or withholds approval, the other device receives no notice. The formal theorem proves the DH-SC message-authentication claim under its ideal authenticated comparison channel; it does not specify what each application returns in those cases. An application cannot infer from a local comparison/approval that the peer application committed success. Classification: **TERMINAL AGREEMENT REQUIRES NEW PROOF**. Adding a final confirmation or changing success semantics is not covered by Theorem 1.

## Focused P2 follow-up — context-to-key binding

### Required property and terminology

Let `Context` denote the semantic, role-ordered tuple for one ceremony: the expected peers and roles; ceremony identity; protocol/domain and profile version; each participant's opaque application-context input; and any identity or public-key claims returned to the consumer. The tuple is deliberately semantic here; this research does not choose an encoding.

For honest parties `A` and `B`, let `sid_A` and `sid_B` be their local Candidate C session identifiers, `K_A` and `K_B` their outputs, and `Context_A` and `Context_B` their local semantic contexts. The required agreement event is: whenever both sessions accept, are partnered under Candidate C's relation, and are claimed as the same successful ceremony, then `K_A = K_B` and `Context_A = Context_B`; each acceptance must correspond to a unique honest peer session. The context/key bad event is an accepting result that associates a key from one Candidate C run with a context or approval from another, or two partnered accepting sessions that output the same `K` with unequal context. Candidate C's theorem establishes neither event for context that is outside its protocol input and partnering relation.

The closest established authentication term is **injective agreement on data** (Lowe's hierarchy): when an honest role accepts, a unique peer run must have participated with the expected peer and opposite role and agree on the named data. For this project the agreement data would include the same fresh `K` and `Context`. If uniqueness is omitted, the weaker property is non-injective agreement. AKE key secrecy is a separate property; neither agreement nor secrecy implies the other.

Three claims must stay separate:

1. **Agreed authenticated context:** both accepting sessions agree on the role-ordered `Context` values.
2. **Key-context association:** the proof ties this exact `K` to that agreed `Context`, so an accepting result cannot attach `K` to another context.
3. **Application trust:** the consumer decides what the agreed values mean and whether to authorize anything. Cryptographic agreement does not establish that an opaque claim is true.

P1's intended direction is sound, but its phrase “bind application context” does not say whether it requires agreement, key-context association, or both, and does not name the agreement tuple. **P1 CONTEXT REQUIREMENT IS AMBIGUOUS.** Proposed wording for a later P1 review: “If two partnered sessions both report success, they agree on the same fresh key, reciprocal peers and roles, ceremony, protocol/domain and profile version, and all context/claims described as authenticated. The security argument must state separately whether and how the key is cryptographically associated with that agreed context. Consumer authorization remains an application decision.” This research does not edit P1.

### Candidate C baseline relevant to this question

Jarecki–Saxena's full 2012 version supplies the proof omitted from the SCN 2010 proceedings version. Enc-MCA authenticates two arbitrary strings `m_i` and `m_j` in its SAS channel model. Its three messages commit to `[m_i | s_i | PK_i | R_i]`, encrypt `[m_j | s_j | R_j]` under the initiator's public key, then open the commitment; matching SAS values authenticate the peer's message. It assumes a non-malleable commitment in a common-reference-string model and SS-CCA public-key encryption. Theorem 1 gives a multi-player/multi-session bound in `n`, `τ_t`, and `τ_c`; the authenticated SAS channel preserves integrity and source/target identity but is public and may be delayed, dropped, replayed, or reordered. The formal SAS queue is consume-once, so it does not model duplicate SAS delivery.

Enc-AKA is not the arbitrary-message Enc-MCA with context chosen freely: Figure 2 fixes `m_i = null`, and the responder chooses fresh uniform `K` as `m_j`. Theorem 2 proves indistinguishability of the output key for partnered sessions with bound `2 n τ_t τ_c (2^-k + max(ε_C, ε_E))`, under the same commitment/encryption assumptions and static corruptions. The output is `(Peer, K, sid)`. A session has local `init`/`resp` role and intended `Peer`; sessions partner only for reciprocal peers, complementary roles, and equal SIDs. Each SID is computed as a collision-resistant hash of the messages sent and received in that run, including SAS messages (the authors note `[s_i | s_j]` also suffices for their argument). The theorem relies on fresh nonces and explicitly accounts for concurrent sessions; the result is not forward-secret.

The internal SID contains the protocol transcript, not the consumer's separately supplied context. Peer and role values participate in the *partnering relation*; they are not a general proof that an application identity claim or public-key ownership claim is true. There is no theorem parameter for arbitrary context, and the paper does not quantify over arbitrary SIDs that an application may safely redefine. **Adding context to Candidate C's SID: NEW PROOF REQUIRED.** Treating an external label as part of a reported SID without changing the protocol does not authenticate it; changing the partnering SID changes the theorem's security game and freshness/reveal relationships.

#### Candidate C mapping to the generic BR composition theorem

Brzuska, Fischlin, Warinschi, and Williams prove in Theorem 1 that a Bellare–Rogaway-secure key exchange with an efficient public session-matching algorithm can be composed with any secure symmetric-key protocol, with a reduction to the two component advantages. A secure symmetric-key protocol could in principle express mutual authentication of role-ordered context messages and acceptance only after each side checks the peer's value. This is the strongest generic composition result found that could bridge key exchange to post-exchange context agreement.

| Theorem requirement | Candidate C property | Match? | Evidence / remaining gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| BR key secrecy for key distribution `D` | Enc-AKA outputs fresh uniform `K`; Theorem 2 gives a test-key bound for completed partnered sessions under static corruption. | **PARTIAL** | Theorem 2 is stated in the SAS-AKA game, with an authenticated public SAS side channel and its own test/reveal rules. No reduction equating this game to the exact BR game required by BFWW11 is given in the paper or project evidence. The side-channel assumption cannot be silently dropped or treated as plain BR security. |
| Efficient public session matching | Candidate C exposes roles, intended peers, network transcript, and SAS deliveries; its SID is derived from the messages of each run. | **PARTIAL** | These values appear sufficient to attempt a public matcher from the adversary's history, and the paper says fresh nonces make each partner unique except with negligible probability. A matcher satisfying BFWW11's “correct after every game response” definition has not been constructed or proved against the exact SAS-send model. |
| Symmetric-key protocol secure under `D` | The context exchange would need to authenticate each role's context under the session key, reject unequal expected values, and associate acceptance with the same KE session. | **UNKNOWN** | BFWW11 Theorem 1 assumes a separately secure `π`; it does not define or prove this context-agreement functionality or an implementation for Candidate C. A proof of `π` and its session/key interface is still required. |
| Composition preserves concurrent sessions and their matching | Candidate C's direct theorem counts total and concurrent sessions; BFWW11's reduction counts participants and all composed sessions. | **PARTIAL** | The parameters and freshness/reveal conditions must be mapped jointly, including Candidate C's `τ_t`, `τ_c`, static corruption, and the `π` sessions. **BFWW11 result: relevant but not yet applicable.** Its theorem is not, by itself, a published Candidate C context-binding result. At least the base-game equivalence and the secure-`π` premise remain unproved for this exact composition. Applying the result would therefore require an explicit new composition proof or a source theorem with these SAS-channel premises; no protocol messages or cryptographic construction are selected here.

**Context-transform result: NEW PROOF REQUIRED.** No reviewed primary source gives a theorem that takes Candidate C as proved and, without an additional proof step, gives injective agreement on its same `K` and an independently supplied two-party `Context`. A MAC of `Context` under key material derived from `K` is a plausible route to agreement, but would be a new composition argument; it is not asserted as an established transformation here.

### KDF and channel-binding distinction

A secure KDF can preserve indistinguishability of derived keys when its secret input meets the KDF's entropy/security premise and its public `info`/context is independent of that secret. Public `info` need not be secret and may be attacker-visible; any attacker influence still has to meet the independence premise. Changing public context should produce computationally separated derived key material under the KDF's PRF guarantee, with the corresponding collision/advantage terms. That statement alone does not tell either side which context the other used. If `Context_A ≠ Context_B`, deriving different local keys only causes an explicit context failure if an authenticated check/key-confirmation step covers the same context. A KDF also cannot upgrade an attacker-controlled, unauthenticated identity, role, version, or ceremony label into an agreed fact.

FGSW Theorem 5.1 derives fresh confirmation and session keys from the AKE key with its KDF, then authenticates the AKE SID. It requires key secrecy of the source AKE and adds KDF and MAC security losses to the base assumptions (with reductions depending on the number of sessions); it does not feed arbitrary public application context into the KDF. RFC 5869 describes `info` as a means of separating derived material by application/context and requires it to be independent of IKM. TLS 1.3's exporter adds a label and context under its specified key schedule. These are key-derivation facts, not a proof of application-context agreement or rejection on mismatch for Candidate C.

Channel binding is similarly scoped: it lets an upper-layer mechanism bind its authentication to a particular lower-layer channel value under a specified interface and lifecycle. RFC 5929 defines TLS bindings and warns about applicability/negotiation details; RFC 9266 defines `tls-exporter` for TLS 1.3 and requires unique master secrets. The RFC 9266 introduction explicitly says TLS 1.3 channel-binding safety was not analyzed as part of core TLS 1.3 work. Neither RFC is a generic composition theorem for a SAS-AKE.

### UKS and cross-context failure evidence

The common UKS/misbinding root cause is that a key or authenticated exchange is not cryptographically tied to the identity/context the consumer later associates with it, or that the user-intended peer is not mapped to the authenticated cryptographic peer. This is distinct from an attacker learning `K`.

| System | Missing binding | Attack/result | Lesson for Candidate C |
|---|---|---|---|
| STS-MAC; Blake-Wilson–Menezes, PKC 1999 | Peer identity is not consistently tied to the key-confirmation context | Published unknown-key-share attacks let one participant hold a key while attributing it to a different peer | Reciprocal `Peer` and role matching in Candidate C address the abstract partner relation; this does not authenticate external identity claims. |
| TLS 1.2 triple handshake; Bhargavan et al., IEEE S&P 2014; RFC 7627 | Handshake/session uniqueness was insufficiently bound across resumption/renegotiation; channel-binding values could coincide across distinct connections | Attackers synchronized sessions and broke applications' assumptions about client authentication and unique channel binding without breaking the underlying primitives | A transcript-like/channel-binding value is only as strong as its exact handshake, resumption, and lifecycle model. Candidate C's SID is transcript-derived within one modeled ceremony, not a durable app-context binding. |
| EAP methods; Hoeper–Chen, QShine 2007 | Three-party/two-link method, cipher-suite, and channel-binding relationships were not reliably bound | Attacks made channel binding, protected negotiation, and cryptobinding claims fail, affecting key exchange, authentication, and privacy | Protocol/domain/version and negotiated choices must be in the proven binding; their presence in UI or a separate message is insufficient. |
| Bluetooth SSP and EAP-NOOB bootstrapping; Sethi–Peltonen–Aura, AsiaCCS 2019 | The user's physical pairing intent was not bound to the ephemeral key/actual peer | Implemented identity-misbinding attacks paired a victim with a third device; formal analysis found a double-misbinding variant | Even a valid SAS/comparison can bind the wrong devices if the user's intended endpoints are not bound to the key. Candidate C's abstract peer labels do not prove physical identity. |

For Candidate C, the paper's peer IDs, opposite roles, and SID prevent a partner from being declared under a different *modeled* peer, role, or transcript. This does not cover unknown real-world identity, OOB user intent, application context, or higher-layer labels absent from those values. The paper does not prove context-sensitive UKS resistance for added inputs.

### Key confirmation, SID, and Enc-MCA composition checks

- Confirmation of `K` over Candidate C's existing SID can support key confirmation under the mapped FGSW assumptions, but it cannot distinguish two external contexts omitted from that SID. It must not be described as context confirmation.
- Including `Context` only in a local success/result object is not authentication. Including it in the SID changes the formal session relation; appending it to confirmation/KDF inputs is a composition not stated in the cited theorem. A theorem must show both key secrecy and agreement, not just local derivation.
- Enc-MCA can authenticate arbitrary `Context_A` and `Context_B` as messages under Theorem 1. Theorem 2 does not establish that a successful Enc-AKA `K` is attached to those messages: its proof tests partnered Enc-AKA sessions and derives the key result from its fixed `null/K` construction. No common SID/partnering theorem composes a separate Enc-MCA acceptance with the key output. Separate context and key executions could therefore be spliced at the application layer without contradicting either separate theorem.
- Theorem 2 already handles interleaving and concurrent sessions with `τ_t` and `τ_c`; it does not include a new context authentication execution in those bounds. A separate execution would need a joint session/partner definition and a reduction accounting for its sessions, matching, key secrecy, and context agreement.

### Minimal context that carries security meaning

The P1 threat model does not justify authenticating every opaque application byte. The narrower set below is the minimum security-relevant meaning identified by this review; it is not a proposal to weaken P1 silently.

| Field | Why it matters | Candidate C coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Protocol/domain and profile version, including any security-relevant negotiated choice | Prevents one protocol or weaker/different profile from being interpreted as another. | **NO.** These values are not Enc-AKA inputs in the theorem. |
| Reciprocal peer and initiator/responder roles | Defines which parties and message directions the agreement concerns. | **YES, within the formal model.** They constrain matching/partnering; this does not establish real-world identity or proof of possession. |
| Exact protocol run/transcript | Prevents protocol messages and key outputs from being paired across runs. | **YES, within the formal model.** Fresh nonces and transcript SID support unique partnering with negligible collision probability; this does not bind human approval or provide a durable restart-safe ceremony record. |
| Human approval event and local ceremony handle | Ensures approval authorizes only the exact active ceremony, including retries and restarts. | **NO.** Candidate C does not model UI approval, callbacks, or persistent terminal state. |
| Context inputs or identity claims that change authorization or are represented as authenticated | Their mismatch changes the meaning of the accepted pairing result. | **NO.** Enc-MCA can authenticate messages separately, but Theorem 2 does not bind them to its key. |
| Friendly display names or UI labels with no security meaning | They do not identify a peer or alter the cryptographic result. | **Not required in the cryptographic core.** Consumers may present them as unverified metadata and must not treat them as authenticated identity. |

Thus arbitrary metadata can remain application policy, but narrowing the P1 requirement to only the minimum security-relevant set does not close P2: protocol/version and security-relevant context still need a justified binding, while Candidate C only covers its own formal peer, role, and transcript relation.

### Formal proof obligation if no existing composition applies

**Given.** Enc-AKA Theorem 2 establishes fresh-key indistinguishability for partnered sessions under non-malleable commitment and SS-CCA encryption assumptions, static corruption, and explicit total/concurrent-session bounds. Its SAS channel is public, authenticated, and adversary-schedulable. Enc-MCA Theorem 1 separately authenticates arbitrary messages under its theorem assumptions. Neither result joins an Enc-MCA context acceptance to the Enc-AKA `K` result. FGSW Theorem 5.1 supplies key confirmation on the existing SID; BFWW11 Theorem 1 is a generic composition lead, but its Candidate C premises remain unmapped.

**Desired theorem.** In the combined protocol and application-result model, except with probability bounded by the Candidate C primitive/SAS advantages plus explicitly stated composition and context-authentication advantages, every pair of honest partnered sessions that both accept and report the same ceremony has equal fresh `K`, reciprocal peer and roles, and equal role-ordered security-relevant `Context`. No accepting output may associate a key from one run with context or approval from another. The statement must preserve the stated concurrency limits and Candidate C's secrecy result.

**Adversary wins if.** There exist honest accepting sessions for which (a) partnered outputs have `K_A = K_B` but `Context_A != Context_B`; (b) a local result attaches `K` from `sid_A` to context/approval belonging to a distinct `sid_B`; (c) the same accepted result is attributed to a different peer, role, domain/version, or ceremony; or (d) one peer session justifies multiple distinct successful ceremonies beyond the theorem's negligible collision bound.

**Extension under consideration.** Only the abstract requirement that the key-session partnering relation, authenticated context agreement, and accepted key/context result refer to the same unique session. No fields, encoding, messages, primitives, or protocol changes are specified here.

**Missing theorem and obligations.** Either prove that BFWW11's exact BR-security and public-matching premises follow from Candidate C's authenticated-SAS game and that a separately secure context-agreement protocol composes under the same `K`, or provide another published theorem whose premises Candidate C demonstrably meets. The proof must cover key secrecy; authenticated agreement on context, peer, and role; injectivity and session/key matching; cross-session/key/context splice resistance; cross-protocol and version separation; concurrency and session-bound loss terms; interaction with reveals/static corruption; and role-specific completion under message loss. KDF output separation alone, separate Enc-MCA success, and local approval state do not discharge these obligations.

**Counterexamples to account for.** UKS attacks on STS-MAC; TLS Triple Handshake and the EMS/channel-binding repair; EAP channel-binding and protected-negotiation failures; and implemented Bluetooth SSP/EAP-NOOB misbinding and double-misbinding attacks. These are failure patterns, not evidence that Candidate C itself is broken.

### P2 decision and contradiction search

**Context-binding result: NEW PROOF REQUIRED.** **P2 outcome: RESEARCH CONTINUES.** Candidate C remains the best justified direct key-agreement construction, but the one open P2 proof question is whether a proof or established composition binds both participants' generic application contexts to Candidate C's same fresh `K` while preserving its secrecy, authentication, and concurrency result. The separate terminal-outcome item remains a P1 wording ambiguity, not another P2 proof blocker: under the proposed clarification above, local completion may differ under message loss, and partnered sessions that both succeed must agree. P3 stays gated. No construction or primitives are selected.

**Contradiction search.** The strongest contrary evidence is FGSW Theorem 5.1: a generic, proof-backed post-AKE transform authenticates a session identifier under fresh KDF-derived MAC keys and provides role-asymmetric key confirmation. It does not include an external context input, and Candidate C's proved SID excludes one; redefining its SID or adding a context-authentication step needs a joint proof. BFWW11 Theorem 1 goes further by composing BR-secure key exchange with arbitrary secure symmetric-key protocols; it could be a bridge to context agreement if Candidate C and a context-agreement protocol meet its exact premises. The BR game equivalence and secure context protocol have not been proved for Candidate C's authenticated-SAS model, so the theorem is not currently applicable. Krawczyk's HKDF analysis and RFC 5869 support context-dependent derived outputs, but KDF security is not agreement/authentication and does not reject a caller mismatch on its own. TLS 1.3 proves a protocol-specific transcript/SID-to-key relationship, while the Triple Handshake demonstrates the failure when session/channel-binding uniqueness is assumed beyond its actual lifecycle. Enc-MCA's arbitrary messages remain the closest Candidate C component, but the separate theorem does not join its accepted messages to Enc-AKA's tested key. These results do not contradict **NEW PROOF REQUIRED**.

### Proposed P1 clarification (not applied)

P1 should distinguish the following requirements rather than use “context binding” as one undifferentiated claim:

- **Agreed authenticated context:** partnered successful sessions agree on the role-ordered semantic context and claims authenticated by the protocol.
- **Key-context association:** the proof shows that the same pairing key cannot be accepted as belonging to a different agreed context. State whether this is obtained through session agreement/key confirmation, derivation, or another proved property; do not infer it from a separate authenticated context message.
- **Application trust:** the consumer interprets the agreed context and makes authorization/trust decisions. Pairing does not validate the truth of consumer-supplied claims.
